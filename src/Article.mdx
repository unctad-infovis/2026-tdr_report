  #### Global trade surpassed $35 trillion for the first time in 2025 despite conflicts and rising trade restrictions, and is expected to remain resilient in 2026, even as a major energy shock adds to these pressures. But that resilience is fragile and uneven. Growth is slowing, financial risks are mounting and development finance is under strain.

  The rules of the game are changing. Governments are using trade restrictions, financial subsidies and technology controls to compete for industries they see as vital to economic and national security, including semiconductors, artificial intelligence infrastructure and clean energy technologies.

  The report calls this <strong>geoeconomics</strong> – the use of economic tools to pursue strategic goals – and it's redrawing the global trade and investment map. Trade remains global, but access is becoming more selective.

  Apart from a handful of Asian economies, most of the global South is falling further behind. A country can attract investment, supply critical minerals or assemble goods for export and still capture little of the value created, as foreign firms often control the most profitable stages of production.

  Yet the changing map of production also creates openings. As companies shift operations, some developing economies are attracting new trade and investment. Their rise is also widening markets for others.

  The question is whether developing economies can turn these openings into stronger industries and lasting growth.

    **Global growth is projected at 2.6% in 2026 and 2.7% in 2027, down from 2.9% in 2025. Growth in developing economies is expected to slow from 4.7% in 2025 to 4% in 2026.**

    That uneven resilience faces three pressures: the energy shock, risks in the AI boom and shrinking development finance.

    Higher energy prices fuel inflation. Central banks may raise interest rates in response, but that can't restore lost supplies. The shock has exposed the cost of energy dependence and adds urgency to diversifying energy sources. Since 2024, renewables have been cheaper than the least expensive new fossil-fuel power in more than 90% of cases.

They made up about one sixth of global goods trade by value in 2025 and accounted for 42% of its growth. If demand for AI hardware slows, goods trade could lose a major source of momentum.

    **Trade remains global, but access is becoming more selective. Policy increasingly shapes partner choices.**

    Since 2015, goods have travelled farther between trading partners, showing that companies still source what they need from suppliers far from home where cost and scale make sense. But since 2021, a growing share of global trade is taking place between geoeconomically compatible countries as geoeconomic risk and policy uncertainty weigh more heavily on sourcing decisions.

    <strong>Trade shifts can create new opportunities.</strong> Trade reconfiguration is creating new opportunities for some developing economies. United States imports from China fell 24.3% between 2024 and 2025, while those from the rest of East Asia rose 23.3%. Africa gained 83 export relationships with Europe and 74 within Africa from 2019 to 2025. Each relationship represents more than $1 million in goods exports between two countries.

    But turning new trade links into lasting gains requires stronger industries, institutions and regulatory capacity.

    <strong>Market access is becoming more costly and complex.</strong> Alongside shifts in trading partners, tariffs are raising the cost of reaching markets. The global average duty on manufactured goods rose from 1.9% in 2024 to 4.7% in 2025, reflecting increases in one major economy. Duties on imported components can also raise manufacturers' costs.

    But the direct cost of duties is only part of the picture. Over the past 45 years, exports from least developed countries grew by 7.6% in periods when trade-policy uncertainty and geopolitical tension were both low, compared with 2.4% in high-uncertainty periods.

    Even when tariffs are low, exporters face a growing range of trade measures. Governments in both developed and developing economies are reporting more technical product, health and safety requirements.

    Environmental requirements are also becoming more prominent, including rules on carbon emissions and deforestation. Firms may meet the underlying standards yet still be shut out if they can't provide the required evidence – a particular burden for smaller exporters.

    <strong>Common rules and regional cooperation can widen market access.</strong> For developing economies, the World Trade Organization's non-discrimination rules can make market access more predictable and less dependent on bargaining power.

    Regional agreements can also widen markets and lower trade barriers. In Africa, full implementation of the African Continental Free Trade Area could raise regional income by 7% and lift 30 million people out of extreme poverty by 2035.

      - <strong>Help firms meet new trade requirements</strong> by investing in certification, product tracking and digital customs systems.
      - <strong>Protect fair access to global markets</strong> and ensure developing economies have a voice in setting trade rules.
      - <strong>Help developing economies build lasting export markets</strong> through export promotion, market information, finance and stronger regional and South–South cooperation.
