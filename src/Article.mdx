    #### Energy shock hits poorer households hardest

    The Middle East conflict disrupted energy supplies, sending crude oil from $70 to over $110 a barrel. Poorer households feel the squeeze most.

    Higher energy prices fuel inflation. Central banks may raise interest rates in response, but that can't restore lost supplies. The shock has exposed the cost of energy dependence and adds urgency to diversifying energy sources. Since 2024, renewables have been cheaper than the least expensive new fossil-fuel power in more than 90% of cases.

    #### AI boom lifts trade and raises financial risks

    Global trade has held up better than expected given the risks. Trade in goods and services grew 4.4% in real terms in 2025 and is projected to expand about 4% in 2026. Since March 2026, higher prices have driven much of the registered growth in trade values.

    But growth rests on a narrow base. AI-related goods, including chips and servers used in data centres, have become the main driver of merchandise trade. They made up about one sixth of global goods trade by value in 2025 and accounted for 42% of its growth. If demand for AI hardware slows, goods trade could lose a major source of momentum.

    The AI boom shows signs of earlier financial bubbles. A fall in share prices could trigger selling and squeeze credit well beyond the technology sector.

    Its gains are highly concentrated, too.

    #### Less aid, more volatile capital flows

    Development aid is shrinking as financial volatility rises. It's projected to fall 6.9% in 2026, marking a third straight annual decline. Since 2022, aid has also shifted away from developing regions.

    {/* FIGURE I.17 – Aid could fall to 0.23% of donor income, far below target.     Meanwhile, amid global uncertainty, capital flows to developing countries became more volatile in 2026. When investors pull back, borrowing costs can rise, leaving governments with less room to respond to shocks or invest in public services and infrastructure.

Geoeconomic risk and policy uncertainty are weighing more heavily on firms' sourcing decisions.

    #### Trade shifts can create new opportunities

    The global trade map is shifting. Trade between **China** and the **United States** has fallen by more than 20% since 2024, while **East Asia** has expanded trade with both China and North America.

    New trade links are emerging elsewhere, too. **Africa** gained 83 export relationships with **Europe** and 74 within Africa from 2019 to 2025. But turning new links into lasting gains requires stronger industries, institutions and regulatory capacity.

    #### Market access is becoming more costly and complex

    Alongside shifts in trading partners, tariffs are raising the cost of reaching markets. The global average duty on manufactured goods more than doubled from 1.9% in 2024 to 4.7% in 2025.

    {/* FIGURE II.2 – Global manufacturing tariffs more than doubled in 2025. Average tariffs weighted by the value of goods traded, agriculture, natural resources and manufacturing, percentage, 2024–2025. */}
    <ChartDataWrapper chart_id="suIeN" />

    Even when tariffs are low, exporters face a growing range of trade measures. Governments in both developed and developing economies are reporting more technical product, health and safety requirements.

    Environmental requirements are also becoming more prominent. Firms may meet the underlying standards yet still be shut out if they can't provide the required evidence – a particular burden for smaller exporters.

    #### Common rules and regional cooperation can widen market access

    For developing economies, the World Trade Organization's non-discrimination rules can make market access more predictable and less dependent on bargaining power.

    Regional agreements can also widen markets and lower trade barriers. In Africa, full implementation of the **African Continental Free Trade Area** could raise regional income by 7% and lift 30 million people out of extreme poverty by 2035.

      - <strong>Protect fair access to global markets</strong> and ensure developing economies have a voice in setting trade rules.
      - <strong>Help developing economies build lasting export markets</strong> through export promotion, market information, finance and stronger regional and South–South cooperation.

    Developed economies mainly use financial tools such as grants, state loans and rules on foreign investment and cross-border financial flows. Developing economies outside China rely more on trade policy.

    #### Exports don't guarantee local value

    Mineral wealth alone can't guarantee local gains. The **Democratic Republic of the Congo** produced about 73% of the world's mined cobalt in 2025, but domestically owned producers accounted for just 1% of its output. **Africa** captures less than 1% of the value in green supply chains.

    {/* FIGURE III.17 – Local producers account for just 1% of Democratic Republic of the Congo's cobalt. Cobalt mine production in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, domestic artisanal and small-scale producers and multinational large-scale producers, percentage, 2020–2025. */}

    **Mexico** shows a similar gap between exports and local value. Its technology and electronics exports more than doubled, from $45 billion in 2000 to $110 billion in 2025, but 70.7% of the value was created abroad.

    **Malaysia** has built a major semiconductor industry. Electrical and electronic shipments account for 44.3% of the country’s merchandise exports. But the most valuable parts of the industry remain concentrated in established semiconductor powerhouses, limiting Malaysia’s ability to move up the value chain.

    #### Regional cooperation can widen opportunities

    Rising economies in the global South are emerging as anchors of regional cooperation in trade, technology and payments.

    In **Latin America**, **MERCOSUR** has developed a system for cross-border trade payments in local currencies. **India** has agreements with 23 countries to share its digital public infrastructure, with technical cooperation already operating in eight. The Association of Southeast Asian Nations' (**ASEAN**) payment network links eight countries. In **Africa**, the Pan-African Payment and Settlement System aims to connect 42 currencies.
